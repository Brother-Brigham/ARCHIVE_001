**Terms**:

1. Separation of Powers/Checks and Balances: power is shared/divided between government branches so no branch can acquire too much power
Canada's separation of powers is more limited. Unlike the US, Canada's executive and legislative powers are combined in the Parliament. https://www.ourcommons.ca/marleaumontpetit/DocumentViewer.aspx?Sec=Ch01&Seq=2
2. Executive: Enforce law
    1. Head of State: The top representative of a state _to other_ states. 
Canada's head of state is the British monarch, and is represented in Canada's government by the Governor General. This differs from the US president, who is both head of state and head of government. https://www.ourcommons.ca/marleaumontpetit/DocumentViewer.aspx?Sec=Ch01&Seq=2
    2. Head of Government: The top policy-maker _within_ a country
Canada's Prime Minister is the head of government. Unlike the US President, the Canadian Prime Minister is not elected; rather, they are the member of parliament (usually a party leader) that is able to maintain the confidence of the majority of the House of Commons. https://www.ourcommons.ca/marleaumontpetit/DocumentViewer.aspx?Sec=Ch01&Seq=2
    3. President: Head of state, can also be head of government. Usually directly elected by the public
Mexico's president is directly elected by the people for a single, six-year term. Comparably, Mexico's president exercises similar powers to the US president. https://www.britannica.com/place/Mexico/Government-and-society
    4. Semi-Presidential: a state has a president and a prime-minister
France uses a semi-presidential system. The president is elected, similarly to the US president, but the president then appoints a prime minister, unlike in the US. https://www.elysee.fr/en/french-presidency/the-institutions
3. Executive AND Legislative: where members of the executive are, _first and foremost,_ members of the legislative
    1. Prime Minister: usually leader of the largest party in the legislature, indirectly elected. Head of government, often functions as head of state. 
Canada's prime minister is the leader of the political party that holds the highest number of seats in the House of Commons after a federal election. The US does not use this kind of system. https://electionsanddemocracy.ca/parliament/canadas-political-system
    2. Coalition government: No party gains a majority in the legislature, so two or more parties will cooperate to create a "majority". 
Germany's Bundestag, a directly elected parliament, appears to be comprised of five "parliamentary groups" or parties and a number of "non-attached members". The number of parties would require a coalition government, whereas a coalition will likely never be necessary in the US because of its two-party system. https://www.bundestag.de/en/members
4. Legislative: Create law
    1. Parliament. most countries have a parliament where the US has Congress.
Canada's legislative body is a Parliament. The Parliament is composed of the British monarch (or Governor General), the Canadian Senate, and the Canadian House of Commons. The US legislature does not include a monarch or other head of state, instead separating that function into the executive branch. https://www.ourcommons.ca/marleaumontpetit/DocumentViewer.aspx?Sec=Ch01&Seq=2
    2. Unicameral legislature. Only one legislative, like if the US had only the House of Representatives
New Zealand's parliament is unicameral, consisting only of a House of Representatives. The US, by contrast, uses a bicameral legislature with a Senate in addition to the House of Representatives. https://www3.parliament.nz/en/visit-and-learn/how-parliament-works/our-system-of-government/
	3. Bicameral legislature. Two legislative bodies, lower and upper, like the US House and Senate.
Canada's parliament is a bicameral system, consisting of the Senate and the House of Commons. Comparably, the US uses a Senate and a House of Representatives. However, where the Senators are elected in the US, Senators in Canada are appointed by the Governor General to represent the provinces and territories. https://www.ourcommons.ca/marleaumontpetit/DocumentViewer.aspx?Sec=Ch01&Seq=2
5. Judiciary: Interpret law
    1. Constitutional Court: The courts who decide if laws are constitutional
Germany seems to have a separate federal constitutional court. In the US, this role is performed by the Supreme Court, which also handles appellate questions. https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/EN/TheFederalConstitutionalCourt/TasksOrganisation/tasksorganisation_node.html
	2. Criminal Court: The courts who decide if someone broke the law
Ontario's criminal court handles most federal criminal statute violations in Canada. Similarly, the majority of criminal violations are handled in state courts in the US. https://www.ontariocourts.ca/ocj/criminal-court/    
	3. Civil Court: The courts who decide if someone didn't necessarily break the law, but if someone's behavior inhibited someone else's ability to live in a manner to which they are accustomed.
The Ontario Superior Court of Justice is one example of a civil court in Canada. Canadian civil courts seem to function identically to civil courts in the US. https://www.ontariocourts.ca/scj/filing-procedures/rules/civil/
